# SimpleAlgorithms

The author's hand-written math estimates (a square root, sine and cosine from a Taylor series, a factorial, a power and a division) and a Tower of Hanoi move printer. Original Eclipse source from the author's own files (only `src/` was kept).

It started as a console program that printed a few results. It still prints them, then opens the **Algorithm Lab**: a window that shows each algorithm working, step by step.

## Controls / usage

On start, `main` prints the original output, in order:

- `sqrt(42) ~= ...` (Newton's method)
- `sin(pi/4) ~= ...` and `cos(pi/4) ~= ...` (4 Taylor terms each, about 0.70711)
- `5! = 120` and `5^3 = 125.0`
- each step of the division search, then `4/2 = ...`

Then the Algorithm Lab window opens. Its tabs (click them, or press 1 to 8) are:

- **Sorting race**: six sorts (bubble, selection, insertion, merge, quicksort and heapsort) on the same numbers, side by side. The window opens on it, already racing. Each tick every sort gets the same number of steps: a compare or a write of one value is one step, a swap is two. Bars light orange for a compare and red for a swap or write. When a sort finishes, a green check runs along its bars and it gets its place (1st for the fewest steps; equal runs share a place). At the end the page says which sort won, in how many steps, and how many times as many the slowest took. Each sort shows its compares and writes, and how its work grows for random numbers (n² or n log n).
  - Click a sort to follow and listen to it (Quicksort at first): each tick plays a vibraphone note pitched by the value it just touched, and the green check plays a rising run. M: sound on or off.
  - The line under the title is about the sort you follow. While the race plays it says how that sort works (Quicksort "takes the last number as the pivot, puts smaller ones before it, then sorts each side."). Paused, it says what that sort's last step did and why, with the numbers and places (`30 is not smaller than the pivot 26, so it stays on the big side.`), and the bars it touched carry those numbers on tags. Step forward (S) and back (Left) to read a sort's reasoning one move at a time. Each sort writes its reason down as it records the step, so the line always matches what it did.
  - **Bars | Prints** (beside the title, or V): Prints draws each sort's whole run as one picture instead of bars. A column per place in the list and a row per moment of the race, from the start at the top to the end of the slowest sort at the bottom (100 rows, on the race's shared step clock); each number keeps its color, from blue for the smallest to red for the largest, so a finished sort shows as a smooth blue-to-red band. The picture grows downward while the race runs, and a dark line marks the clock. Each sort leaves its own shape: bubble sort's diagonal stripes, as each pass carries the largest number left over to the right end; selection sort's sorted edge sliding in from the left; merge sort's sorted blocks growing wider as halves merge; heapsort's tangle that then settles from the right. Press (and drag) on a picture to move the race to that moment, paused, playing the note there for the sort you listen to; the bars show that moment too, and Play goes on from it.
  - Space: Play or Pause (Again once all are done, on the same numbers). S or Right: one tick's step, paused. Left: one step back, paused. R: the same numbers again. N: new numbers. O: the order (Random, Nearly sorted, Reversed, Few values: four heights). B: 16, 32, 64 or 128 numbers. F: speed (Slow, Normal, Fast, Turbo; the speeds are for 32 numbers and scale with the count, so a race takes about as long).
  - Some things it shows: insertion sort wins on nearly sorted numbers and bubble sort stops early there; quicksort here takes the last value as its pivot, so reversed or few-valued numbers make it slow; selection sort writes little (at most one swap per place) however many compares it makes.
- **Hanoi** (Tower of Hanoi): plays the moves `Hanoi.moves` produces (the same list `Hanoi.solve` prints) on 1 to 8 disks. For each move it shows the move number in binary: the lowest 1 bit names the disk that moves, which is the idea the loop is built on, and the line `solve` prints for it (`Move 2 left`). Click a peg to lift its top disk and another to drop it; a disk can't go on a smaller one. After a hand move the page says how many moves are left at best and what the best next move is, and Step and Play finish the puzzle from there. When solved it says whether that took the fewest possible moves (2^n - 1).
  - Space: Play or Pause. S or Right: one move. B or Left: one move back, paused (a disk in flight goes back to its peg, a lifted one is put down, and a hand move taken back returns to the printed path if it left it), all the way to the start. R: reset. `-` and `+` (or `=`): fewer or more disks. F: speed (Slow, Normal, Fast, Turbo).
- **Square root**: steps through `MyMath.squareRootSteps`, every guess `estimateSquareRoot` makes. Each guess a is drawn as an a by n / a rectangle, whose area is always n; averaging its two sides makes the next guess, so the rectangle turns into the green square of side sqrt(n). A table lists the guesses with their correct digits in green. Type a number (up to 12 characters) to change n; Backspace deletes.
  - Space: Play or Pause. S or Right: next guess. B or Left: previous guess. N: next example (42, 2, 10, 1000, 0.5, 123456789).
- **Sine & cosine**: plots `estimateSine` or `estimateCosine` with 1 to 10 terms (red) against the real curve (green), with the sums of fewer terms fading behind. Point at the plot (or click it, or use Left and Right) to pick x; the side lists each term at x, their sum, the real value and the error. It opens on sin(pi/4) with 4 terms, the line the original program prints.
  - Space: Play (adds one term at a time, from 1 to 10). `-` / Down and `+` / Up: fewer or more terms. C: sine or cosine. P: back to x = pi/4.
- **Pathfinding**: a 29 by 19 grid you draw on, and five ways to search it for a way from the start (S) to the goal (G): breadth-first, depth-first, Dijkstra, greedy best-first and A*. Steps go up, down, left or right; a step onto open ground costs 1 and onto mud 5. The page plays the picked search cell by cell: pale blue cells were looked at early and deeper blue ones later, yellow cells are seen but not yet looked at, orange is the one being looked at, then the way it found is drawn in red. Beside the grid a table runs all five on the same map and lists how many cells each looked at, and the steps and cost of its way, with the best in each column in green; a line under it says how the picked search works. Drawing reruns every search at once, so the table and the drawn way change while you drag.
  - Drag on the grid to draw walls (or mud: D switches the Walls button to Mud); pressing a cell that already is one rubs it out. Drag S or G to move them. Click a search in the table, or press A, to pick it and watch it from the start.
  - Space: Play or Pause (Again once done). S or Right: one cell, paused. B or Left: one cell back, paused (a drawn way goes first). M: a new random maze (with some loops and mud patches). X: the example map. C: clear the walls and mud. F: speed (Slow, Normal, Fast, Turbo).
  - The example it opens on shows the differences: the goal sits behind a cup that opens toward the start, and a band of mud lies between them with a dry way round at the top. Breadth-first finds the fewest steps (36) by wading through the mud (cost 48); Dijkstra and A* walk round it (38 steps, cost 38), A* after looking at 184 cells to Dijkstra's 454; Greedy looks at the fewest cells but walks into the cup and through the mud; depth-first wanders 98 steps.
  - The map and the picked search are saved in `~/.simplealgorithms-paths.txt` (one line per row: `.` open, `#` wall, `~` mud, `S`, `G`), so a drawn map is still there next time.
- **Division**: steps through `MyMath.divide`, the search behind the `4/2 = ...` line. It counts: it adds 1 to its guess until guess * b passes a, steps back 1, counts on by 0.1, passes a again, steps back, counts by 0.01, and stops once a step back is smaller than 0.01. Each step size gets its own ruler, and each ruler is a tenfold zoom of the one step above it where the count passed a (shaded, with lines down to the next ruler). Every guess is a hop along its ruler (red when guess * b passes a, grey arching under for a step back, green for the answer), a green mark shows the real a / b, and beside the rulers are the lines `divide` prints, with `returns` and the answer last (the last step back is not printed). The two lines under the rulers multiply the guess out against a and say what happens next. It opens on 22/7 (3.14).
  - Type a division (digits, `.` and `/`, for example `22/7`; up to 12 characters, a and b above 0, a / b at most 100); Backspace deletes.
  - Space: Play or Pause. S or Right: next step. B or Left: previous step. N: next example (22/7, 4/2 as the original program prints it, 7/2, 1/3, 100/7, 2/3).
  - **Code** (C, again for Rulers): the rulers give way to `divide` itself, as the author wrote it (printing its steps), and each step lights the lines that ran in that pass of its `while` loop: the counting-on `else` or the step-back `if`, with the set-up lines on the first step and `return` on the last. Beside the lit lines are what they did: the test (`21.7 != 22`), the new `guess`, `tolerance` and `counter2` on a step back (tolerance goes 1, 0.1, 0.01, 0.001, which is all `counter2 * error` is for), `counter`, and under the step-back test the gap `currentGuess - guess`. That gap is how the loop knows to stop, and it shows the rounding behind it: on 22/7 and 7/2 the last step back is `0.00999999999999979 < 0.01`, so it stops at hundredths. The lines `divide` printed stay in their column beside the code.
  - Some things it shows: tenths do not add up exactly in a `double`, so 7/2 counts to 3.5000000000000004, which times 2 is a hair more than 7, and it ends on 3.4999999999999982 rather than 3.5. Whether it stops at hundredths (22/7 gives 3.14) or goes on to thousandths (1/3 gives 0.333) is the same kind of rounding: the step back from 3.15 to 3.14 comes out a hair under 0.01, from 0.34 to 0.33 a hair over.
- **Weigh** (sort by weighing): here you are the sorting algorithm. 3 to 8 boxes (6 at first, lettered A to H) look alike and have different hidden weights, from 10 to 99 g. Click two boxes (or press their letters) to put them on the balance: it tips toward the heavier one. Esc takes boxes off the pans before the second one is on.
  - The left of the page draws what your weighings prove: a line runs from a box to a heavier one, and each box stands in the column of the longest chain of boxes proven lighter than it, so a box moves right as you learn it is heavy. A proof through a chain counts: once B < D and D < C are weighed, B < C is known without weighing it, and weighing it anyway is listed as "nothing new".
  - The side counts the orders of the boxes, lightest first, that still fit your weighings (720 for 6 boxes at first), with a bar that fills by the information found: each weighing that halves the orders adds the same length. No weighing can do better than halve them in the worst case, which is why "always enough" is log2 of n! rounded up: 3 for 3 boxes, 5, 7, 10, 13 and 16 for 8 (for these counts that many weighings always suffice, with the right method). The last four weighings are listed with the orders before and after each.
  - When only one order is left the boxes stand in one green row and open to show their weights, and the side sets your count against the compares merge sort, insertion sort and bubble sort (the race's sorts) make on the same boxes in their starting order. A lucky run can beat "always enough", and so can a sort.
  - **Hint** (?) rings the unweighed pair whose weighing leaves the fewest orders however it tips, and says how many each way. **Weigh for me** (Space) weighs that pair. In the self-check's trials, weighing the hint every time sorted 3 to 7 boxes within the "always enough" count and 8 boxes within one more. **Show weights** (W) peeks (the result then says so). **New boxes** (N), **Fewer** (-) and **More** (+ or =) deal new boxes.
- **Printout**: the lines `main` printed at start, as a terminal shows them. H adds what `Hanoi.solve(3, true)` prints (`main` has a commented-out call to `solve(5, true)`).

On the Square root and Division tabs, the digit keys type the number, so switch tabs there by clicking.

## Code layout

- `src/simplealgorithms/Main.java`: entry point; prints the results above (`printout()` builds the lines) and opens the Algorithm Lab.
- `src/simplealgorithms/MyMath.java`: square root (`squareRootSteps` lists every guess), sine and cosine (one shared Taylor series), power, factorial, and add/subtract/multiply/divide built from simpler operations. `divide` can hand its steps to a callback instead of printing them.
- `src/simplealgorithms/Hanoi.java`: `moves` is the original move loop, collecting `Move`s (disk, right or left); `solve` prints them (`Move <disk> left/right`).
- `src/simplealgorithms/HanoiTower.java`: the pegs, legal moves, and the shortest solution from any position (`nextMove`, `movesLeft`).
- `src/simplealgorithms/Sorts.java`: the six sorts and the four orders of numbers. Each sort runs on its own copy and records every compare, swap and write as a step, with a note saying why (`describe` fills in the numbers).
- `src/simplealgorithms/SortRace.java`: plays the recorded steps of every sort against one step clock, works out the places, keeps each sort's print (its values at 100 moments of the race) and moves the clock to any moment (`seek`).
- `src/simplealgorithms/SortPage.java` and `SortSound.java`: the Sorting race tab, and its notes on the MIDI synthesizer (opened on a background thread; silent without one).
- `src/simplealgorithms/Grid.java`: the Pathfinding map (open, wall and mud cells, start and goal), the example map, the maze maker, and saving.
- `src/simplealgorithms/Paths.java`: the five searches. Each records the order it looked at cells in and when it first saw each one, so the page can replay it.
- `src/simplealgorithms/PathPage.java`: the Pathfinding tab.
- `src/simplealgorithms/Weighing.java` and `WeighPage.java`: the Weigh tab's boxes (what the weighings prove, the orders still possible, the hint's pair) and the page.
- `src/simplealgorithms/LabPanel.java`: the window's graph-paper panel: tabs, the button bar, and routing of clicks, drags, keys and a 30 ms tick to the page showing.
- `src/simplealgorithms/Page.java`: base class of the eight pages, with the shared colors, fonts and number formatting.
- `src/simplealgorithms/DividePage.java`: the Division tab; `steps` reads the lines `divide` prints back into hops, step backs and the answer, and replays `tolerance` and `counter2` for the Code view, which shows `divide`'s source (`SOURCE`).
- `src/simplealgorithms/HanoiPage.java`, `RootPage.java`, `TaylorPage.java`, `PrintoutPage.java`: the other four tabs.
- `src/simplealgorithms/Conversions.java`: empty class.

## Running

From the repo root: `./run.ps1 old SimpleAlgorithms` (or `./run.ps1 old SimpleAlgorithms -Build` to build a jar).

## Self-check

`test/simplealgorithms/MyMathCheck.java` checks the sine, cosine, square root, factorial, power and division results against `java.lang.Math`. `test/simplealgorithms/HanoiCheck.java` plays `Hanoi.moves` for 1 to 10 disks and checks every move is legal, matches the shortest solution and ends with the tower on the right peg, and that from scrambled positions the solver finishes in exactly `movesLeft` moves; and that the page's Back takes back a hand move onto the printed path, then every move to the start. `test/simplealgorithms/PathCheck.java` runs the five searches on 300 random maps and checks each finds a way exactly when there is one, never looks at a wall or a cell twice, and that its way is a real one whose cost adds up; that breadth-first finds the fewest steps and Dijkstra and A* the cheapest way (against a slow search that tries every step until nothing improves), with A* looking at no more cells than Dijkstra; the lessons of the example map above; that 100 random mazes all have a way through; and that a saved map loads back. `test/simplealgorithms/SortCheck.java` plays back each sort's recorded steps for every order and several counts and checks they sort the numbers, checks the lessons above (insertion sort wins on nearly sorted numbers, merge sort beats bubble sort by far on random ones, quicksort is slow on reversed ones), and that the race gives every sort the same steps and places the cheapest first; that every step's note fills in (and reads right for a few small cases: a bubble sort compare and swap, quicksort's pivot compares, a merge); that each print starts on the numbers and ends sorted, and that moving the clock to a row's moment (forward or back, and past either end) gives the values that row shows. `test/simplealgorithms/DivideCheck.java` checks the Division page's reading of `divide` on 22/7, 4/2 and 7/2 (which passes 3.5 by a hair), and on about 2,000 random divisions that every step is one hop of its ruler's size, that only a guess that passes a is marked as passing it, that each step back drops one size, at most to 0.001, and that the last step is what `divide` returns, within 0.01 below a / b; and that the Code view's `tolerance` replays `divide` exactly (each guess is the one before plus or minus it, to the last bit), with `counter2` divided by ten on each step back. `test/simplealgorithms/WeighCheck.java` checks the "always enough" counts for 1 to 8 boxes, the orders left on a small case, that a chain proves the pair at its ends (and weighing that pair again proves nothing), the columns, and in 300 trials of 3 to 8 boxes that weighing the hint every time sorts without a wasted weighing, in at most one more than "always enough", with each box in the column of its rank by weight. ## Fixed bugs

- `Hanoi.solve` always printed `Move 0 right` for the smallest disk. That only works for an even number of disks: with an odd number the other lines then name moves that are not allowed (for 3 disks the second line, `Move 1 right`, puts disk 1 on disk 0). The smallest disk now follows the same rule as the other even-numbered disks, so every tower ends on the right peg.
- `estimateSquareRoot` looped until the guess equalled n / guess exactly, which never happens for 0 (the guess halves down to 0, and 0 / 0 is NaN, which equals nothing). It now stops after 100 guesses.

## Known quirks

- `factorial` returns a `long`, so it overflows past 20!. Sine and cosine need at most 19! at the lab's 10 terms.
